# 3D NACA 0012 wing: mesh study (Quick / Standard / fine)

Generated by `scripts/mesh_study_wing3d.py` on 2026-11-03 (aarch64 Linux container, 4 MPI ranks, one run at a time). Raw numbers: `wing3d_mesh_study.json`. **Uncalibrated prototype - not yet validated.**

Case: the app's default wing, NACA 0012, chord 0.3 m, span 0.6 m (aspect ratio 2, flat tips), 20 m/s, Re = 4.11e+05, alpha 0 and 10 deg; the app's `external_aero` template (blockMesh + snappyHexMesh with 5 boundary-layer cells, Spalding wall function, simpleFoam k-omega SST, fully turbulent, Tu 0.1 %) and its everyday stop rule. The three mesh levels are one refinement family: only the background cell changes (quick 2 c, standard 1.29 c, fine 0.645 c; the wing surface is refined 7-8 levels below it, so the surface cell is 1/128-1/256 of the background cell), all refinement levels, boxes and layer settings stay the same.

## Where the drag at 0 deg comes from

Flat-plate estimate at Re = 4.11e+05, fully turbulent from the leading edge: Cf = 0.00574 (ITTC-57) or 0.00531 (Prandtl-Schlichting); form factor 1.252 (Hoerner, t/c = 0.12); wetted area 2.040 x planform for the surfaces plus 0.082 x planform for the two flat tip caps. That gives **CD0 = 0.0151 (ITTC-57) / 0.0140 (Prandtl-Schlichting)**.

## Conclusion for students

**Lift is the number to trust.** At 10 deg the Quick mesh gives CL = 0.451; the finest mesh here (fine) is 0.455, so Quick is -0.9 % off (3 mesh levels). That is the same size as the solver's own error against NASA's 2D reference (about 2 %), and far smaller than the difference between two airfoils or two angles. Drag at 10 deg: Quick 0.0558 against 0.0516 (the Richardson-extrapolated value), +8.2 %; at this angle most of the drag is induced drag (about CL^2 / (pi e AR) = 0.036), which the mesh captures well because it comes from the lift distribution, not from the boundary layer.

**Drag at zero lift is where Quick is weakest.** Quick gives CD = 0.0171 at 0 deg: +9.4 % above Richardson extrapolation (0.0156). The split shows why: the skin-friction (viscous) part, 0.0107, barely changes with the mesh and agrees with a flat-plate estimate (0.0122 with ITTC-57), while the pressure part, 0.0064 on Quick, falls as the mesh is refined (0.0042 on fine). The pressure drag of an attached airfoil comes from how the boundary layer thickens towards the trailing edge, and the Quick mesh's 5 layer cells (about 5 mm) cover only half of that boundary layer; the rest sits in coarse cells.

**How much of the gap is mesh, and how much is physics?** The validated 2D section, run wall-resolved at the same Reynolds number, gives CD = 0.0125 (pressure 0.0024, viscous 0.0100); adding skin friction on the two flat tip caps gives about 0.0129. Against that, Quick is +32 % high and the mesh-converged 3D value still +21 % high (the remainder is the wall-function mesh family itself plus whatever the tip edges really add). All of these assume a boundary layer that is turbulent from the leading edge. Measured NACA 0012 drag near Re = 4 x 10^5 in low-turbulence tunnels is about 0.009-0.011, roughly 20 % below even the wall-resolved fully turbulent value, because a real boundary layer stays laminar over a good part of the chord. So of the gap between Quick (0.0171) and a measurement (about 0.010): roughly 59 % is mesh and wall treatment and 41 % is the fully turbulent assumption.

**Should Quick stay the default?** Yes. It runs in about a minute on 4 cores; the finer levels cost standard 2-5x the time and 2 GB, fine 9x the time and 6 GB for a change of about 1-2 % in lift and 5-8 % in drag. Use Quick for what the course needs - lift curves, comparisons between shapes, angles and sizes, and the pressure pictures - and read its absolute drag as an upper estimate (and L/D as a lower one). Differences between runs on the same mesh are far more reliable than the absolute drag of any one run.

The fine run at 0 deg was stopped by hand at iteration 2560 (writeNow) after CD had been constant to five digits for over 500 iterations: the app's stop rule had not fired because the spanwise-velocity residual (normalised by an almost-zero field at zero incidence) stayed at 1.4e-4, just above its 1e-4 threshold; the run would otherwise have gone to the 3000-iteration cap. Its runtime therefore overstates what a settled fine run needs.
Memory: snappyHexMesh needed 5.8 GB for the fine mesh and simpleFoam 6.5 GB; a 12 GB WSL2 allowance would take it, but a 16 GB laptop would not run anything else meanwhile.
Quick-preset variants tried (2026-11-03): (a) surface refinement level 8 everywhere instead of 7-8: 709k cells, i.e. nearly the Standard mesh, so no longer a Quick run; (b) 8 boundary-layer cells instead of 5 (stack 7.0 mm instead of 5.5 mm, +21 % cells, +19 % runtime): CD at 0 deg 0.01724 (pressure 0.00617, viscous 0.01107) against 0.01711 with 5 layers, CD at 10 deg 0.05484 against 0.05578, CL unchanged. The thicker stack lowers the pressure part by 4 % but raises the viscous part by the same amount, so the total does not improve: the Quick error is in the surface cell size (and the y+ it sets), not in the layer count. Neither variant was adopted; the Quick preset is unchanged.
Stop rule fixed on 2026-11-03 (task 007): the residual guard now looks only at pressure, k, omega and the velocity components that carry freestream (windlab.case_builder.residual_guard_fields), so the spanwise residual no longer blocks it. Replayed on the recorded histories, the new rule stops the fine 0 deg run at about 350 iterations and the standard one at about 330, both with CD already at its long-run value (tests/test_convergence.py); a fresh Quick 0 deg run stopped at 329 iterations (CD 0.01711, 65 s).
