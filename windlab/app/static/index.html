About WindLab

SenSym WindLab is a virtual wind tunnel for engineering students: it meshes a part, runs the open-source OpenFOAM® solver on your own computer and shows the lift and drag. Uncalibrated prototype — not yet validated. For educational and preliminary design use; not for airworthiness or safety determinations.

                    What do these numbers mean, and how much can I trust them?

                    CL and CD are the lift and drag forces divided by the dynamic pressure of the wind (½ρV²) and the reference area, so they do not depend on the size of the part or the speed, only on its shape, its angle to the wind and the Reynolds number. L/D is simply their ratio. The pitching moment Cm (under More details) is taken about the reference point, the quarter-chord for the built-in wings. The "wobble" is how much CL and CD still moved over the last solver iterations: it tells you whether the calculation settled, not whether it is right.

                    Accuracy. The flow solver itself was checked in 2D against NASA's NACA 0012 reference at Re 6 million: lift within 2 % and drag within 1 % of the reference. The Quick mesh you are using is much coarser. On the built-in NACA 0012 wing it gives lift within a few percent of what a finer mesh gives, but drag about 9 % too high (8 % at 10°) compared with what the same method gives on a mesh ten times finer, and about a third above the validated 2D calculation at the same Reynolds number, because the surface cells are coarse (see the mesh study in the project's validation folder). On top of that, WindLab assumes the boundary layer is turbulent from the leading edge; at the Reynolds numbers of a student-size model (10⁵ to 10⁶) real air keeps a laminar run first, so measured drag is lower again, often by a third. Treat CD as an upper estimate and L/D as a lower one, and compare runs made on the same mesh with each other: trends and differences are far more trustworthy than absolute drag. A part that is symmetric top to bottom has zero lift at 0°; a small CL shown there is numerical noise from the mesh, not physics. Thin fins or plates thinner than a mesh cell would make it large, so WindLab measures the thickness of every uploaded part and refines the mesh around such parts on its own (the "Thin parts" note under the mesh choice says when).
